How apps score pronunciation

Language apps have scored pronunciation for years. This is a short tour of how they do it, what a version of that costs in a browser, and why none of it comes ready-made for Sanskrit.

The core idea: goodness of pronunciation

A speech model listens to the sound and says, for every moment, how likely each speech sound is. The classic score, Goodness of Pronunciation or GOP, takes the likelihood of the sound that should be there over the stretch where it was heard, and compares it with the best competing sound. A low score means the model heard something else. Two recent papers adapt it to the newer models: one makes it work without a separate alignment step (Cao et al., arXiv 2507.16838, July 2025), and one limits it to plausible confusions (Parikh et al., Interspeech 2025, arXiv 2506.02080).

For a sense of how good such detection is on English learners' speech: a wav2vec2 system reached an F1 of 0.610 (Xu et al. 2021) and a training-free retrieval method reported 69.6 % (arXiv 2511.20107, November 2025). The data splits differ, so these are not a leaderboard.

The candidate for tier 2: ZIPA-small

ZIPA-small is a 64-million-parameter multilingual phone recogniser (ACL 2025, arXiv 2505.23170), licence CC-BY-4.0. The 8-bit version is 70.7 MB (16-bit 131.6 MB, 32-bit 260.3 MB; repository updated 2026-10-06). It outputs 127 speech-sound symbols including retroflex, aspirated and long sounds. It was trained on Hindi, Marathi, Bengali, Telugu, Tamil and others, and not on Sanskrit. On one laptop thread it ran at 0.042 times real time: 0.55 seconds for a 13.2 second verse.

Because the verse is known, the plan is not to recognise Sanskrit at all. The verse's transliteration is turned into the expected sounds by a fixed table of about 40 rules, the model's output is aligned to them, and each syllable gets a GOP. The score used is the drop compared with the reference take, which cancels sounds the model always hears differently (on clean takes the median drop was 1.34 nats, a unit of log likelihood).

What it gets wrong on Sanskrit, as observed on the two clips read: ś and ṣ both came out as the same sound (ʃ), and dhū came out as d̪oː, the aspiration lost. That this is general is a hypothesis. The model's greedy output for Day 2 verse 1 begins:

hoːmmkratʃtʃaːmrakʃaːt̪umeːtaːrakaːmaruːpaːnivaːsiːni...

What runs on a phone and what runs on a laptop

  • The model runs in the browser through onnxruntime-web 1.31.1 (MIT). It uses WebAssembly, or WebGPU where there is one. Multi-threading needs a page setting that GitHub Pages does not give without a workaround (hypothesis).
  • WebGPU (web.dev, 2025-11-25): Chrome and Edge 113 and later on desktop; Chrome 121 and later on Android 12 and later with some graphics chips; Firefox 141 and later on Windows and 145 and later on Apple-silicon Macs, not Android or Linux; Safari 26 on iPhone, iPad and Mac.
  • An iPhone tab is killed, not warned, when it uses too much memory: "Only ~300 MB of RAM is reliably available to WebAssembly pages" (WebKit bug 269777). ZIPA-small at 8 bits fits. The 240 to 630 MB models do not, so they are for laptops only.
  • The browser speed of ZIPA is not measured. The hypothesis is 2 to 3 times slower than the native run: 1 to 2 seconds per 12 second take on a laptop and a few seconds on a phone.

So on a phone, tier 1 is certain and tier 2 is plausible. If there is no WebGPU, memory is low, or the download is declined, the tool falls back to tier 1 alone.

What this can and cannot tell you

It can tell you what exists, what it costs and what the report recommends. It cannot tell you that tier 2 works on a phone, or on any voice but one: neither has been measured.
